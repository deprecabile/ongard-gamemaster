# Day-Night Cycle of Ondgard

## Basic Principles

Ondgard rotates on its axis in **32 hours**. The axial tilt of ~23° produces 4 seasons that modify the length of
daylight according to latitude, exactly as on Earth but with dilated proportions.

At the **equinox**, day and night each last **16 hours** at all latitudes. At the solstices, the difference between
light and dark becomes more pronounced the further one moves from the equator.

---

## Time Reference (32-Hour Clock)

The day is measured on a 32-hour scale. At the equinox, the cardinal points of the day are:

| Event    | Time  |
|:---------|:------|
| Midnight | 0:00  |
| Dawn     | 8:00  |
| Noon     | 16:00 |
| Sunset   | 24:00 |

The **peak heat** is reached in the late afternoon, about 2–3 hours after solar noon (~18:00–19:00), due to the
thermal inertia of the dense atmosphere. The **thermal minimum** occurs just before dawn.

---

## Seasonal Variation by Climate Belt

The table shows approximate dawn, sunset and daylight hours at the solstices.

### Equatorial Belt (0°–35°)

Even at the equator, seasons influence the length of daylight, though modestly.

| Season          | Dawn  | Sunset | Hours of Light |
|:----------------|:------|:-------|:---------------|
| Summer solstice | ~7:30 | ~24:30 | ~17 h          |
| Equinox         | 8:00  | 24:00  | 16 h           |
| Winter solstice | ~8:30 | ~23:30 | ~15 h          |

### Golden Belt — Subtropical (35°–55°, reference ~45°)

The most inhabited belt. Well-defined seasons but never extreme.

| Season          | Dawn   | Sunset | Hours of Light |
|:----------------|:-------|:-------|:---------------|
| Summer solstice | ~5:30  | ~26:30 | ~21 h          |
| Equinox         | 8:00   | 24:00  | 16 h           |
| Winter solstice | ~10:30 | ~21:30 | ~11 h          |

**Practical example — Summer in the Golden Belt:**
Dawn arrives at 5:30. The sun climbs slowly through the orange sky for nearly 10 hours, reaching the zenith around
16:00. Heat builds until 18:00–19:00 and then fades gradually. The long, crimson sunset begins around 24:30 and full
darkness falls only after 27:00. Night lasts barely 10–11 hours: brief, mild, often lit by the glow of Astro or the
auroras of Shinosa.

**Practical example — Winter in the Golden Belt:**
Dawn is delayed until 10:30. The sun remains low on the horizon and sets by 21:30, granting only 11 hours of golden,
dim light. The long 21-hour night cools the air, but the dense atmosphere and oceans limit the thermal excursion to
8–12 °C.

### Upper Temperate Belt (55°–75°, reference ~60°)

Extreme seasonal contrast.

| Season          | Dawn   | Sunset | Hours of Light |
|:----------------|:-------|:-------|:---------------|
| Summer solstice | ~3:30  | ~28:30 | ~25 h          |
| Equinox         | 8:00   | 24:00  | 16 h           |
| Winter solstice | ~12:30 | ~19:30 | ~7 h           |

In summer the sun sets at 28:30 and rises again at 3:30 the next day: true night lasts less than 7 hours and the sky
never fully darkens (perpetual twilight). In deep winter, daylight lasts barely 7 hours, with late dawns and early
sunsets.

### Polar Cap (beyond 75°)

Beyond the polar circle, the **midnight sun** (summer) and the **polar night** (winter) occur, each lasting several
weeks depending on latitude.

---

## Extended Twilights

Ondgard's slow rotation stretches the transitions between day and night. Dawn and sunset each last **2–3 hours**
(compared to ~30–40 minutes on Earth). Light changes gradually: the sky shifts from the pale daytime blue to shades of
amber, garnet and deep violet before darkness.

This extended twilight is an important ecological window: many crepuscular species (neither diurnal nor nocturnal) are
active exclusively during these transition hours.
